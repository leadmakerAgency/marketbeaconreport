The Toronto Stock Exchange and the S&P 500 are two of the most referenced benchmarks for North American equity investors, but they are not the same animal. This briefing explains how the TSX and the S&P 500 differ by sector, why that matters for returns and risk, and what signals investors tend to watch when weighing the two exposures.

The difference matters because sector mix drives how an index reacts to economic cycles, commodity prices, interest rates and currency moves. Understanding the tilt of each index helps investors assess where concentration risk and opportunity lie, without offering any specific investment instructions.

## What the two indexes represent
The S&P 500 is a market-capitalisation-weighted index of large US-listed companies, and it is dominated by large-cap technology and other growth-oriented sectors. Over time the index’s performance has reflected the earnings trends of a relatively small number of very large firms and the sectors that lead innovation and consumer demand in the US.

The TSX (a shorthand for the Toronto Stock Exchange composite index) is dominated by financials and resource companies. Canadian banks, insurance companies, oil and gas firms and a range of mining companies make up a large share of the market capitalisation listed in Canada. That gives the TSX a different economic sensitivity compared with a US large-cap index.

## How the sector mixes differ
The main structural contrast is straightforward. The S&P 500 has a heavy weighting in information technology and in sectors that benefit from global consumer and enterprise demand for digital products and services. That makes the S&P 500 more tied to trends in software, semiconductors, online advertising and other technology-driven revenues.

The TSX leans more towards finance and natural resources. Financials include large banks and insurers, which are sensitive to interest-rate spreads and credit conditions. Resource sectors include energy and materials, which are sensitive to commodity prices and global demand for raw materials. That resource tilt means the TSX often tracks commodity cycles and the Canadian dollar more closely than the S&P 500.

## Why it matters for investors
Different sector mixes mean different drivers of returns. A tech-led rally in the US can lift the S&P 500 even if commodity prices are flat, while a strong cycle in oil, base metals or higher interest margins can support the TSX even when US growth stocks are under pressure. Currency moves also matter, because many Canadian companies earn revenue in US dollars or global prices, while their stocks trade in Canadian dollars.

Sector concentration also affects income profiles. Resource and financial stocks often pay dividends or have payout policies that make the TSX yield profile different from a tech-heavy index where dividends tend to be lower. Investors who care about income, inflation sensitivity or cyclicality should be aware of these structural differences.

> **The takeaway:** The TSX’s finance and resources tilt makes it more sensitive to commodity prices, interest-rate cycles and the Canadian dollar, while the S&P 500’s technology and growth concentration ties it more to innovation-led earnings and large-cap US demand.

## The risks and structural biases
Sector composition creates predictable biases in risk exposures. Here are the main ones to watch:

- **Concentration risk:** The TSX and the S&P 500 each concentrate market power in a handful of sectors, which can exaggerate volatility when those sectors swing.  
- **Cyclicality:** Resource sectors are cyclically sensitive, so the TSX can underperform when commodity demand softens and outperform when demand strengthens.  
- **Interest-rate sensitivity:** Financials react to changes in interest rates and yield curves, so shifts in monetary policy can favour or penalise TSX banks versus S&P 500 tech firms.  
- **Currency exposure:** Movements in the Canadian dollar change the domestic return profile of internationally exposed Canadian companies, adding an extra layer of risk or diversification.

These structural biases are not inherently good or bad, but they are sources of persistent divergence between the two indexes.

## How investors typically respond
Investors who want North American equity exposure choose among options that reflect these differences. Common approaches include holding a broad US large-cap allocation to capture the S&P 500’s growth tilt, adding a Canadian equity allocation for exposure to resources and financials, or combining ETFs for a balanced sector mix.

Some investors use the differences strategically. For example, portfolios that aim for higher dividend income may prefer a larger TSX weighting, while growth-oriented investors may favour US large caps. Others view the TSX as a commodity-sensitive complement to US tech exposure, using the two indexes together for broader diversification.

Keep in mind that switching between exposures brings transaction, tax and tracking considerations. Rebalancing between index exposures is a decision about portfolio design rather than a timing bet on short-term market moves.

## What we're watching
- **Commodity price trends,** because rising or falling oil and base metal prices tend to move the TSX more than the S&P 500.  
- **Interest-rate direction and yield curves,** which influence bank net interest margins and the relative appeal of financial stocks.  
- **Currency moves for the Canadian dollar,** since a stronger or weaker CAD affects domestic returns for exporters and resource firms.  
- **Earnings composition in the S&P 500,** especially whether large-cap technology companies continue to account for a big share of aggregate earnings growth.
