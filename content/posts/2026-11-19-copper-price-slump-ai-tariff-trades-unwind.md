Copper has just had its roughest stretch in months. On Wednesday the December Comex contract fell as much as 6% at one point, its steepest one-day drop in eight months, according to Investing.com and IndexBox. That leaves it about 14% lower in three weeks and roughly 17% below the record it set in September. In London, three-month copper touched US$12,640 a tonne, a seven-month low.

Most of this year's gain is now gone. At the peak copper was up 22% for 2026. After this week, it is up about 1%.

## Why it's falling

No single thing broke. Several props that had been holding the price up gave way at the same time.

- **The AI trade is coming apart.** Many investors bought copper as a way to play the data centre build-out, since every new facility needs a great deal of wiring and power equipment. As doubts about AI spending grew, those positions were sold. Nvidia beat its own revenue forecast this week and copper fell anyway.
- **The tariff premium is fading.** Traders spent much of the year shipping copper into the US ahead of a possible tariff on refined metal. TD Securities now says that tariff looks less likely. US warehouses hold about 85% of the 1.09 million tonnes in exchange-monitored stocks, and with prices higher in China, some of that metal may now head back out.
- **A stronger US dollar.** The dollar is near a 19-month high on expectations of more Federal Reserve rate rises, which makes copper dearer for buyers outside the US.
- **The funds are heading for the door.** Speculative net long positions on Comex have dropped from more than 80,000 contracts six weeks ago to about 35,000, according to Saxo Bank. Billionaire investor Stanley Druckenmiller said this week he is now short copper, meaning he is betting the price falls. Once key chart levels broke, computer-driven funds added to the selling.

## The miners took the hit

Eleven of the largest copper-exposed miners lost a combined US$141 billion in market value over the past month, about 16%, with almost half of that in the last week alone, according to figures reported by IndexBox. Southern Copper fell 22% over the month, Freeport-McMoRan 20% and Glencore 17%. Ivanhoe Mines was the worst of the group, down 30% for the month and 56% for the year.

It reaches beyond share prices. Chile, Peru, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Zambia had all set budgets and currencies against a year of record copper prices.

> **The number:** US$141 billion. That is roughly how much value the big copper miners have lost in a month, on a move in the metal that has mostly been driven by fund positioning rather than mines or factories.

## The strange part: physical copper is tight

If demand had genuinely collapsed, you would expect copper to be piling up. It isn't, at least not in China. The premium buyers pay to import copper into China hit a record US$360 a tonne this week, up from under US$100 in September. Stocks in Shanghai Futures Exchange warehouses fell 18% in a week to 27,642 tonnes, the lowest since December 2023.

Supply is not getting easier either. Chile's copper agency Cochilco expects the country's 2026 output to fall 8.4% to 4.94 million tonnes, its weakest in 23 years. And treatment charges, the fee miners normally pay smelters to process their ore, hit a record minus US$247.20 a tonne earlier this month. A negative fee means smelters are paying miners for the privilege, which tells you how scarce raw material is.

## Two ways to read it

At a copper industry conference in Asia this week, the mood was split. Nicholas Snowdon of trading house Mercuria said current consumption looks closer to a recession than to growth, and Natixis described the mix of US stockpiling and AI money as a bubble. Goldman Sachs took the other side, arguing that the slide is about positioning in AI-linked trades, not weaker fundamentals.

Our read: the paper market and the physical market are telling two different stories, and they can't both be right for long. Either real demand catches down to the futures price, or tight physical supply drags the futures back up. Sentiment is in charge for now.

## What we're watching

- **Chart levels.** Saxo's Ole Hansen sees the next support at US$5.63 a pound, where copper finished 2025, and then US$5.32.
- **China's import premium.** If it stays at records while futures fall, the physical market is winning the argument.
- **The US tariff decision.** A clear no would likely push more US-held metal back onto the world market.
- **Cobre Panamá.** Panama has started formal talks on restarting First Quantum's mine, which would add supply.

The risks run both ways. A deeper slowdown or a wider AI sell-off could push copper lower still, and a squeeze in the physical market could reverse the move just as quickly. For miners and the countries that depend on them, this is a reminder of how much of this year's price was borrowed from expectations.
