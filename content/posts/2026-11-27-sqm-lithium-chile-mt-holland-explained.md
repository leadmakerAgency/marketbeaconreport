SQM, short for Sociedad Química y Minera de Chile, is one of the world's largest lithium producers. Its shares trade in New York under the code SQM and in Santiago. Investors who search for "SQM mining" usually want to know two things: how its lithium business works, and how exposed it is to lithium prices. The short answer to the second question is: very.

This guide explains what SQM does, what its latest results showed and what moves the shares. It is general information, not a recommendation.

## What SQM does

SQM's main lithium source is the Salar de Atacama in northern Chile, a salt flat where lithium-rich brine is pumped into evaporation ponds and processed into lithium carbonate and lithium hydroxide for batteries. The company also produces iodine, specialty plant nutrients and potassium products, but lithium drives most of its earnings when prices are high.

## The Codelco partnership

SQM's rights to the Salar de Atacama were due to expire at the end of the decade, and Chile's government wanted the state to take a bigger role in lithium. The answer was a joint venture with Codelco, Chile's state copper company. That venture, now operating as Nova Andino Litio, runs the Atacama operations and was finalised earlier in 2026.

According to the third-quarter earnings call, the venture produced about 75,000 tonnes in the quarter and expects about 290,000 tonnes for 2026, all from the Salar de Atacama, with guidance of more than 300,000 tonnes for 2027.

## The Australian side: Mt Holland

SQM also owns half of the Mt Holland lithium project in Western Australia, through a joint venture with Wesfarmers. Mt Holland mines spodumene, a hard-rock lithium mineral, and feeds a lithium hydroxide refinery near Perth that is still ramping up.

On the call, SQM said the Mt Holland concentrator is running at or above its design capacity, with 2026 output expected at 175,000 to 185,000 tonnes of spodumene concentrate. It expects attributable spodumene capacity to grow to about 350,000 tonnes by 2030 to 2031. That makes SQM one of the larger foreign investors in the Australian lithium industry.

## What the third quarter showed

SQM reported third-quarter results on 18 November. Net income was US$544 million, down 18% from the second quarter but up about 205% from a year earlier. Results missed analysts' expectations, and the shares fell about 6% after the report, according to Investing.com.

The main problem was price. The average realised lithium price was about US$19.6 a kilogram, down 10% from the previous quarter, and management said fourth-quarter prices will be substantially lower after further market falls. One executive said current prices are below the level needed to bring new supply to market.

> **The number:** US$19.6 a kilogram. That was SQM's average realised lithium price in the third quarter, down 10% in three months, and management expects it to fall further.

## What moves SQM's shares

- **Lithium prices.** By far the biggest driver. Prices respond to electric vehicle sales, battery inventories and new supply from Australia, South America, Africa and China.
- **Volumes.** Growth at the Salar de Atacama and Mt Holland can offset lower prices, up to a point.
- **Chilean policy.** The Codelco venture settled the biggest question, but royalties and state involvement still matter.
- **The Mt Holland refinery,** where a slow ramp-up would delay the hydroxide sales the project was built for.
- **Iodine and fertilisers,** which give some balance when lithium is weak.

## What we're watching

- **Fourth-quarter lithium prices,** and whether the decline flagged on the call deepens.
- **2027 volume guidance** from the Codelco venture and Mt Holland.
- **Australian lithium supply.** If prices stay below incentive levels, higher-cost mines may slow output, which would eventually support prices.
