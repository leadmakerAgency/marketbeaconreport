Gold royalty and streaming companies buy a portion of a mine’s future revenue or metal in return for cash up front. That simple idea changes the risk and return profile compared with running a mine. For investors it means exposure to metal prices and mine success, but with less direct operating risk and smaller capital needs.

This article explains how royalty and streaming deals work, how these companies make money, what investors watch when valuing them, and the main risks to keep in mind.

## What royalties and streams are
A royalty is an ongoing payment tied to a mine’s production or revenue. The owner of the royalty does not run the mine, they collect a share of what the mine produces or earns. Royalties are typically carved into the surface title of a mineral property and persist through ownership changes.

A stream is a contract where the buyer pays an upfront sum, and in return receives a portion of the metal produced or the right to buy metal at a fixed, often reduced, price. Streams are more like a customised supply agreement, and the streaming company usually takes delivery of actual ounces or concentrates rather than cash revenues.

Both structures give investors exposure to a mine’s output while leaving the operator responsible for day to day mining, permitting and capital spending.

## How companies structure deals
Royalty and streaming agreements come in a few common forms. These are the basic structures investors encounter.

- **Net smelter return (NSR):** The royalty holder receives a percentage of the revenue after the mine has processed and sold the metal, so the payment moves with metal price and product quality.
- **Production royalty:** The holder receives a payment based on the quantity of metal produced, regardless of the price received for the metal.
- **Streaming agreement:** The streaming company pays cash up front and then receives a fixed share of metal production or the right to buy metal at a set price per unit.

Deal terms can include thresholds, life-of-mine limits, escalation clauses and dependencies on the mine reaching production. Contracts often specify which metals are covered, how concentrate streams are treated and what happens if a mine expands or changes owners.

## How they make money
Royalty and streaming companies make money in a few straightforward ways. First, they collect recurring payments or deliveries from operating mines. When metal prices rise, NSR and production royalties tend to generate higher cash flows without a corresponding increase in operating costs for the royalty owner.

Second, upfront cash paid for a royalty or stream is an investment. The acquirer expects the present value of future receipts to exceed that upfront cost. If a mine consistently produces and costs are contained, the returns can be attractive because the royalty or stream owner avoids direct mining capital and operating risk.

Third, these companies can build diversified portfolios across jurisdictions and deposit types. Diversification reduces the impact of a single mine failure on total cash flows, smoothing earnings compared with a single operating company.

## The economics investors watch
Investors valuing royalty and streaming companies focus on a handful of repeatable inputs. The two most important are expected future metal production from the portfolio and the assumed long run metal prices. Those drive projected cash flows.

Other inputs include the life of each royalty or stream, any fixed or variable payments per ounce under a stream, and the discount rate used to convert future cash flows into a present value. Analysts also look at portfolio quality, meaning the geological upside of the underlying properties and the financial health and track record of the operators running the mines.

A few practical metrics recur in coverage. Net asset value (NAV) attempts to sum the present value of all royalties and streams. Cash flow per share or a cash yield measure shows current earnings relative to market value. Sensitivity tables are common, showing NAV under different metal price scenarios.

> **The takeaway:** royalties and streams convert mines into financial cash flows, offering commodity exposure with less direct operating risk, but their value depends on future production and metal prices.

## The risks
Royalty and streaming businesses are lower on operating risk, but they face their own distinct set of hazards.

- **Commodity risk:** Changes in metal prices directly affect revenue for NSR and production royalties, and the economics of streams too if the stream price is linked to market levels.
- **Operator risk:** The royalty or streamer depends on the mine operator’s ability to build, run and expand operations; poor execution or cost blowouts reduce receipts.
- **Jurisdiction and permitting:** Royalties and streams are only as good as the legal and permitting frameworks that allow a mine to operate, so political, regulatory and permitting delays matter.
- **Contractual dilution:** Additional encumbrances on a property, or renegotiated terms in distress situations, can reduce future payments to existing royalty holders.

Credit and counterparty exposure is also relevant for streams that include fixed per-ounce purchase obligations. Finally, geological risk remains: a deposit may not produce as expected, or recoverable grades may fall over time.

## What we’re watching
- **Portfolio production profiles:** How much attributable metal a company expects to receive from its royalty and stream portfolio, and updates to those estimates.
- **Operator updates:** Production, cost and expansion reports from the mines that underpin royalties and streams, since miners’ results drive royalty cash flow.
- **Contract activity:** New acquisitions, renegotiations, or the sale of royalty interests, which change future cash flow expectations.
- **Metal-price trends:** Movements in the underlying metals that affect revenue scenarios and NAV sensitivities.
