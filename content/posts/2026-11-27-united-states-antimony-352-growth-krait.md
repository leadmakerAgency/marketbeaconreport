When people ask what the antimony trade looks like when it works, United States Antimony is the usual answer. The Dallas Business Journal recently named it the second fastest-growing company in the Dallas-Fort Worth area, with revenue up 352% over three years, and the only critical minerals company on the list. Behind it, a handful of small explorers are trying to get started. One that has just set out is Krait Critical Minerals (CSE: KRIT, FSE: U0S).

## Why US Antimony is the benchmark

United States Antimony, listed in New York, describes itself as the only fully integrated antimony company outside China and Russia. It runs the only antimony smelter operating in the US, at Thompson Falls, Montana, and holds a sole-source Defense Logistics Agency contract worth up to US$245 million to supply the National Defense Stockpile. It has also secured federal funding to expand its Montana smelter capacity roughly sixfold, according to the company.

The volumes back that up. The company sold 468,842 pounds of antimony last quarter, up 68% on a year earlier, with October its biggest month on record. Prices have been volatile, but demand from Washington has not been the problem.

> **The number:** 352%. That is US Antimony's three-year revenue growth, and a fair measure of what being in the right place at the right time has meant for an American antimony producer.

## Why most antimony juniors don't appeal

The problem for investors looking further down the chain is that many antimony explorers are not especially attractive. A lot of them are older companies with large numbers of shares on issue, heavy warrant overhangs (the right for earlier investors to buy more shares later, which can weigh on the price) and projects that have stalled after an early discovery. A fresh, tidy company with a real project is harder to find.

## Where Krait fits

Krait listed recently and in September closed the acquisition of its US antimony portfolio, upsizing its financing at the same time because of demand. Its main asset is the Bales project in Washington State, which was mined in 1951 and 1952, the last time the US scrambled for domestic antimony. A state survey at the time named Bales one of only three antimony occurrences in Washington with a real chance of commercial production.

Historical samples ran around 10.9% to 14.4% antimony with low arsenic, according to the company, which puts them in a similar range to the material US Antimony is mining today. Bales also sits about 300 road miles from the Thompson Falls smelter, close enough to truck ore to the one buyer in the country that can process it. Krait also holds an option on the Nevada King antimony project in Nevada, where historical shipments graded 41% to 54% antimony.

The company is small and tightly held, with about 17 million shares outstanding according to its latest presentation, and it raised C$1.73 million in October to fund the work ahead. Fieldwork at Bales started within six days of the acquisition closing.

## What could go wrong

US Antimony took years and a smelter to get where it is. Krait has neither yet. The Bales and Nevada King grades are historical and have not been confirmed to modern reporting standards, so they are not a current resource. Exploration can disappoint, permitting takes time, and small companies usually need to raise more money along the way, which dilutes existing holders. Antimony prices also still move sharply with China's export policy.

## What we're watching

- **Results from the Bales fieldwork,** and whether modern sampling and drilling confirm the old grades.
- **Any agreement with a smelter or buyer,** given how close Bales is to Thompson Falls.
- **Washington's next moves** on stockpiling and domestic supply, which set the backdrop for every American antimony project.

US Antimony shows what the destination can look like. Krait is at the start, and the fieldwork now under way is the first real test of whether it can follow.
