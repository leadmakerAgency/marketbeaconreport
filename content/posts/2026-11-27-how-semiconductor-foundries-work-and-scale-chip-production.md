Semiconductor foundries make the actual chips that power phones, servers and cars for other companies. This piece explains how foundries turn designs into silicon at scale, the technical levers they use to increase output, and the economic constraints that shape investment decisions.

If you follow technology stocks or the supply chain, understanding foundries helps you read capital expenditure plans, capacity announcements and why some chips are more expensive or scarce than others.

## What a foundry does
A foundry manufactures integrated circuits on silicon wafers for customers that design chips but do not own fabrication plants. This is called the fabless-foundry model, and it separates design expertise from the heavy physical and capital work of making wafers.

Foundries take a design tape-out, which is the finished blueprints for a chip, and turn it into a mask set used in photolithography. They handle process development, production run planning, quality control and the hand-off to packaging and test facilities that finish the chip.

## The main steps on a wafer
Production starts with a silicon wafer, a thin circular disk that holds many individual chips or dies. Processes are repeated in layers to build transistors and wiring on the wafer surface.

Key operations include photolithography, which transfers patterns from masks to the wafer using light; deposition, which adds thin films of material; etching, which removes unwanted material; doping, which modifies electrical properties; and chemical-mechanical planarisation, or CMP, which smooths layers. After the layers are complete, wafers are cut into individual dies, then packaged and tested.

Explainers for jargon: a die is one discrete chip on a wafer, yield is the share of dies that work correctly, and a process node is a shorthand for a generation of technology that often implies smaller transistors and denser circuits.

## How scaling works
There are two broad ways to scale chip production: improve yield and increase wafer throughput. Yield improvements turn a higher share of dies on each wafer into sellable chips, while throughput means more wafers per tool per day or larger wafers carrying more dies.

Process scaling historically reduced transistor dimensions, letting designers put more function into the same die area. Smaller transistors can lower power and raise performance, but making that happen requires new lithography and materials work. Equipment choices, like advanced photolithography tools, and tighter process control are what enable each new generation.

Wafer size matters too. Moving from a smaller wafer to a larger diameter increases the number of dies produced per run without changing die design. Tool uptime, automation in wafer handling, and factory layout all influence how many wafers a fab can process each day.

> **The takeaway:** foundries scale either by making each wafer more productive through yield and technical improvements, or by processing more wafers through larger tools and better automation, and both paths are capital and expertise intensive.

## Economics and constraints
Foundries are capital intensive because fabrication tools are expensive and complex, and cleanroom space is costly to build and operate. That pushes firms towards high utilisation and long production runs to spread fixed costs across many chips.

Capacity planning is difficult because demand for different chip types changes with product cycles. Customers may need leading-edge processes for high-performance chips, or mature nodes for power devices and sensors. Foundries balance investments between new process nodes and keeping older, profitable lines running.

Other constraints are technical and supply related. Some equipment has long lead times, and specialised chemicals or materials can create bottlenecks. Skilled engineers are essential because small changes in process can have large effects on yield. For investors, those are structural reasons why building or expanding a fab is a long-term commitment rather than a short-term switch.

## Risks and trade-offs
Pursuing the latest process node gives access to the highest-performance markets but costs more in R&D and tools. Staying on mature nodes can be profitable and less risky, but limits the addressable high-end market.

There are also manufacturing trade-offs between die size and yield. Larger dies can do more but are more likely to contain defects, reducing yield. Designers and foundries negotiate around die partitioning, redundancy and error-tolerant architectures to manage that trade-off.

Finally, geographical concentration of advanced fabs creates operational risk. A single natural disaster or supply-chain disruption can affect global supply, because the number of facilities capable of producing at the cutting edge is small compared with demand.

## What we're watching
- **Capacity expansion plans:** announcements about new fabs or tool orders signal where production will grow, and which process nodes the industry expects to need.  
- **Yield improvements:** public commentary about process maturity or reported yield milestones indicates when a node becomes commercially viable.  
- **Equipment supply and lead times:** changes in availability for key tools affect how quickly foundries can scale output.  
- **Customer demand mix:** shifts between demand for leading-edge versus mature-node chips show how foundries will allocate capacity and capital.
