Democrats will control both the House and the Senate from January, and plenty of mining investors have been wondering what that means for Washington's critical minerals push. Our short answer: less than you might fear for the policy itself, and more than you might expect for the companies that have taken government money.

## Why the policy is hard to unwind

Most of the administration's minerals strategy was built through executive action rather than new laws: tariffs, stockpile purchases, fast-tracked permits and direct government stakes in companies. A Democratic Congress can't reverse those without overriding a presidential veto, which needs a two-thirds majority it doesn't have. BMO Capital Markets' chief US economist Scott Anderson made a similar point after the result, saying Democrats will have little power to undo existing law without the White House on side.

There is also broad agreement across both parties on the underlying goal of producing more critical minerals at home and relying less on China. The disagreement is mostly about how much environmental review to trade away for speed.

## What changes: oversight

Controlling Congress means controlling its committees, and committees can hold hearings, demand documents and issue subpoenas. Democrats have said plainly that they intend to use those powers on the administration's minerals deals. Senator Ron Wyden's one-word reaction once the Senate result was clear was "Subpoenas".

There is a lot to look at. Consultancy Project Blue counts more than 160 government-backed minerals deals worth roughly US$40 billion since January 2025, including loans, offtake agreements and equity stakes in companies such as MP Materials, Lithium Americas and Trilogy Metals. In August, 54 House Democrats warned that these loans and investments shift risk onto taxpayers, and Senator Martin Heinrich and Representative Jared Huffman asked the Government Accountability Office to review how agencies handle being both investor and regulator.

> **The number:** about US$40 billion. That is Project Blue's estimate of the government-backed minerals deals done since January 2025, and the pool of money most likely to be examined.

## What it could mean for companies

Investigations don't cancel contracts, but they do cost time and money. Project Blue has warned that probes could raise legal costs for critical materials companies and make some private investors more cautious about co-investing alongside the government. For a small company that has built its story around a federal deal, months of document requests and public hearings are not a comfortable backdrop.

Specific projects are also in the spotlight. Representative Adelita Grijalva has filed a bill to reverse the land exchange for Rio Tinto and BHP's Resolution Copper project in Arizona, which was completed in March. With a Democratic House, bills like that get a hearing they might not have had before.

## The permitting bill that's racing the clock

The one area where the outgoing Congress can still move is permitting. The Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act 62 to 33 on 19 November. Among other things, it sets firmer deadlines for environmental reviews, gives challengers 150 days to sue, limits when permits can be revoked, and lets miners use extra land for waste rock and tailings, closing a gap left by a 2022 court ruling on Arizona's Rosemont copper mine.

It now needs to get through the House before the session ends on 17 December, in a crowded month that also includes government funding. House Speaker Mike Johnson wants to move it, but Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries has called for more negotiation. If it doesn't pass, the new Congress starts from scratch in January, with Democrats holding the pen.

## What we're watching

- **The House vote on permitting** before 17 December. Passing it would lock in faster approvals by law rather than by executive order, which a future president could reverse.
- **The first committee hearings** in January, and which deals and companies they name.
- **Private investment alongside government deals.** If co-investors start holding back, that is the real cost of oversight.
- **Tariffs.** Saxo Bank's Ole Hansen has said the result makes tariffs on refined copper less likely.

None of this changes the direction of travel. Washington still wants more American mines, and both parties say so. What changes is how much scrutiny the money gets, and for companies that have taken government backing, that is worth factoring in.
